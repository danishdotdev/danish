## Categories explain markets. Use cases explain value.

Category claims can be useful shorthand: CRM, project management, AI workspace. But they rarely tell a buyer why a product is worth changing their current behaviour for.

A use case does. It begins with an observable moment of work: after a client call, before a sales handoff, while preparing a recurring report, or when a team is trying to recover context from scattered information.

The difference is practical. “An AI workspace” asks the visitor to imagine the product. “Turn a client call into a reviewed follow-up” gives them something to recognise.

## Start with the job and the trigger

Good use-case-led positioning has three pieces:

- **The person or team:** who has the problem?
- **The trigger:** when does the work become urgent?
- **The outcome:** what becomes easier, faster, safer, or more reliable?

For example: “For client-facing teams, turn the decisions in a call into a reviewed plan before the next conversation.” It is not trying to be everything. It gives the right person a clear reason to look closer.

## Specificity filters in the right people

Founders sometimes worry that a focused message will exclude potential customers. It will exclude some people—and that is a feature early on.

The product needs a group of people who immediately understand the value and can tell you whether it is real. A broad message can attract curiosity without creating useful demand. A specific message lets the right people self-select and gives the team better feedback.

The product can expand later. The first use case is a wedge into a market, not a permanent ceiling.

## Let the product example carry the claim

Once the use case is clear, show the workflow. What information goes in? What does the product produce? Where does the person review or take action?

The example should make the promise credible without requiring a long feature list. It also keeps the team honest: if the product cannot show a meaningful before and after, the positioning may be ahead of the actual experience.

Google’s [ads and landing-page guidance](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6238826?hl=en) emphasises that the message and destination should closely match a visitor’s expectation. The same principle applies even without paid traffic. The headline, product example, and next action should all answer the same use-case question.

## Test the language in real conversations

Say the use-case promise out loud to the people it is for. Notice whether they respond with recognition, a story of their current workaround, or a request for clarification.

If they ask, “What does that mean?” the category may still be too abstract. If they say, “That is exactly what happens after our calls,” you have a better starting point.

Collect the words they use and refine the message. The goal is not a clever tagline. It is a promise that makes the right customer understand what the product will help them do.

## Earn the broader category later

Strong products often become broader over time because one useful workflow reveals adjacent needs. But the way to earn that expansion is by first being clear and valuable in a narrow context.

Lead with the work people already want done. The category can follow once the product has proven it deserves to occupy more of their attention.
