## A prospect list is not a growth strategy

It is easy to build a large list of names. It is much harder—and more useful—to understand why a specific person might care about a specific product now.

The difference shows up in the quality of the pipeline. A row with a job title and an email address is not enough context for a thoughtful message. A good prospect record explains the situation, the relevant work, the reason for reaching out, and the next respectful step.

That makes the pipeline smaller at first. It also makes it more honest and easier to learn from.

## Define the fit before collecting names

Start with a narrow description of the people who are likely to have the problem:

- the workflow they own;
- the trigger that makes the problem urgent;
- the current workaround;
- the team size or environment where the product can help;
- the reason a change would be possible now.

This is more useful than a generic industry filter. A product may be relevant to many companies in theory and still only have a clear first use case for a small group of operators.

## Research for a reason

For each prospective customer, capture only the context that helps answer one question: is there a genuine reason this product could be useful to them?

That might be a public product launch, a visible workflow, an expressed hiring need, a shared challenge, or a connection to a use case you already understand. If there is no reason, do not invent one from a title alone.

The goal is not to assemble personal data. It is to decide whether a message would be relevant enough to justify sending.

## Keep the record useful to a human

A simple early pipeline can include:

- name and role;
- company or team context;
- relevant use case or trigger;
- source of the context;
- the specific problem you think may exist;
- date and outcome of any contact;
- explicit note not to contact again if asked.

These fields help a founder avoid repeated, generic outreach. They also create a useful feedback loop: which kinds of context led to genuine conversations, and which assumptions were wrong?

## Write only when you have a real reason

The first message should be short enough to respect attention and specific enough to be recognisable. Say why you are writing, what you noticed, and what you are trying to learn or offer. Do not pretend to have a relationship you do not have.

If the message is commercial email, it also needs to meet the relevant rules where you operate. In the United States, the [FTC’s CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) explains requirements including accurate header information, non-deceptive subject lines, a clear opt-out mechanism, and honouring opt-out requests. This is general information, not legal advice; consult appropriate counsel for your own jurisdiction and use case.

Respecting those boundaries is not only compliance work. It is product positioning. A message that makes it easy to decline is more credible than one that tries to corner a response.

## Let the pipeline teach you

The result of outreach is not just a booked call. A no, a non-response, or a clarification can all improve the product’s understanding of its audience.

Review the pipeline regularly:

- Which messages led to useful conversations?
- Which use cases created recognition?
- Where did the product promise feel unclear?
- Which segments should be removed rather than pursued further?

A pipeline built this way is not a database of people to process. It is a living record of where the product may be valuable—and where it is not.
