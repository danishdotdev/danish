## Campaigns amplify what is already true

Campaigns are useful when a team knows what message resonates, which people it is for, and what action the product can credibly invite. Before that, a campaign often becomes an expensive way to discover that the language is vague or the offer is not ready.

Conversations are a better starting point because they reveal the work beneath the marketing brief. They show what someone is already doing, where the friction lives, and whether the product’s promise sounds useful in the context of a real day.

## Learn from the workarounds

Ask people what they do now. A workaround is not just a temporary solution; it is evidence of how a problem fits into someone’s workflow.

Listen for details such as:

- the spreadsheet or document they maintain manually;
- the moment they have to chase another person for information;
- the copy-and-paste step they dislike but repeat;
- the risk they are trying to avoid;
- the language they use when explaining the problem to a colleague.

These details help a founder decide where a product can be genuinely useful. They also make marketing more specific, because the message can begin with a problem people already recognise.

The [Nielsen Norman Group’s user-interview guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) supports this approach: grounded questions about past behaviour and context yield more useful insight than abstract opinions about a future solution.

## Make the conversation easy to say yes to

Do not frame an early conversation as a sales call disguised as research. Be clear about what you are trying to learn and why the person’s experience matters.

A simple invitation is enough: “I’m trying to understand how people handle follow-up after client calls. I’m not selling anything on this call; could I ask how you do it today?”

That honesty produces better answers. It also protects the relationship if the product is not yet ready to offer a useful solution.

## Capture the insight in a reusable form

After each conversation, write down more than a quote. Record:

- the specific job the person was trying to complete;
- the trigger that made it urgent;
- the current workaround and its cost;
- the phrases that felt emotionally or practically important;
- the part of your product that would need to be true to help.

Then compare notes across conversations. A single opinion can be interesting. A pattern across people in a similar situation is evidence worth building or writing around.

## Let the message earn its campaign

Once a few conversations produce the same language and the same sharp problem, turn that into a focused page, short demo, or personal message. See whether the right people recognise themselves in it.

Only then does a campaign have a solid job: reach more people who may have the same problem, not manufacture relevance where none exists.

The sequence may feel slower than launching a big campaign. In practice, it keeps the team from scaling confusion. Conversations create the clarity that campaigns can later compound.
