## The copy has to carry the real promise

AI makes it tempting to write bigger claims than the product can keep: “works for you,” “knows everything,” or “never misses a detail.” They sound impressive until a user asks what the feature will actually do with the information in front of it.

Good product copy makes the capability easier to evaluate. It names the job, the context, and the control the person keeps. That is more persuasive than vague confidence because it gives someone a reason to try the feature.

## Start with the outcome, not the technology

“Powered by AI” does not explain a user benefit. Begin with the task that becomes easier.

Instead of: “An intelligent workspace assistant.”

Try: “Turn a meeting into a follow-up draft and proposed tasks.”

The second line helps a person picture the moment they would use it. It also gives the product team a test: if the feature cannot reliably deliver that outcome, the copy needs to become more precise.

## Say what the feature uses

AI output depends on its inputs. Copy should tell people what the feature can draw from, especially in a workspace product.

For example:

- “Create a brief from the selected meeting and account notes.”
- “Suggest CRM updates from this call transcript.”
- “Draft a reply using the conversation you choose.”

That small amount of context reduces uncertainty. It is also an honest signal about the boundaries of the feature. The [Nielsen Norman Group’s guidance on microcontent](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/microcontent-how-to-write-headlines-page-titles-and-subject-lines/) is relevant here: concise labels and headings need to communicate the essential meaning without relying on the surrounding page to rescue them.

## Describe the user’s control

The most reassuring product copy often includes what happens next. Does the feature draft, suggest, or act? Can someone edit it? Is approval required?

Use verbs that match the actual interaction:

- **Draft** when the result is editable text.
- **Suggest** when the product is proposing a choice.
- **Prepare** when the next action still needs review.
- **Send**, **update**, or **create** only when the feature truly completes that action.

The difference is not pedantic. A button labelled “Send reply” carries a different commitment from “Draft reply.” The wording should make that commitment visible before a person clicks.

## Avoid invented certainty

Words such as “perfect,” “always,” “automatic,” and “understands” can create expectations a real product cannot safely meet. Replace them with language grounded in the work:

- “Highlight the decisions and open questions from this meeting.”
- “Propose a follow-up plan based on the notes you selected.”
- “Help you review recent account activity before a call.”

Specificity is not a weaker form of marketing. It is a stronger form of product positioning because it connects the feature to an observable result.

## Let the interface continue the sentence

The promise in a headline should match the label on the button, the empty state, the loading message, and the result screen. If the page says “organise your work” but the first action is “generate summary,” the user has to translate the product’s language for themselves.

The best AI product copy behaves like a clear handoff. It tells a person what will happen, what information is involved, and where they remain in control. That is how a feature earns confidence without asking for blind trust.
