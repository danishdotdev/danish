Teams often try to find their message in a planning document. They gather in a room, list benefits, choose a category, and polish a phrase until it sounds confident. Then they put it on a landing page and wonder why visitors do not act.

The issue is rarely a lack of clever words. It is that the message was decided before the team understood the language of the problem.

A message resonates when someone sees it and thinks, “Yes—that is the situation I am in,” or “That is the outcome I want.” It does not need to be memorable in the abstract. It needs to be recognisable to a specific person at a specific moment.

## Start with the language already in the room

The best raw material is usually already available: customer calls, sales notes, onboarding questions, support tickets, reviews, and the phrases people use when they describe what they were trying to get done. Listen especially for moments of tension. What were they doing before? What felt slow, risky, confusing, or expensive? What changed after they found a workaround?

Do not translate those phrases into marketing language too early. If a customer says, “I lose the thread after every client call,” that is more useful than a generic claim about “streamlining workflows.” Their wording gives you context, urgency, and an implied job to be done.

Collect a small set of exact observations. Then look for patterns across people in the same situation. The objective is not to copy a quote onto a homepage. It is to understand the promise that makes the quote meaningful.

## Write a message as a testable promise

Strong positioning usually contains three parts:

- **Who it is for:** a clear audience or situation.
- **What changes:** a concrete outcome or progress point.
- **Why this approach:** the distinctive mechanism or point of view.

For example, “A workspace for teams” is too broad to test. “Turn client-call notes into the next owner’s task list before the context disappears” is much closer to a usable promise. It tells the right person what changes, and it creates a useful question: do teams with frequent client calls recognise this as a pressing problem?

Make two or three candidate promises rather than trying to perfect one. The variations should differ in meaning, not just adjective choice. One might lead with speed, another with reliability, and a third with a specific use case. If all the options mean the same thing, the test cannot teach you much.

## Test where the audience can respond naturally

You do not need a large ad budget to learn whether language lands. Put a candidate message into a cold email to a carefully chosen group, the opening of a sales call, a landing page for one campaign, or the first screen of an onboarding flow. Then watch what people do and ask.

The right evidence is not just clicks. It includes the questions a prospect asks back, the examples they volunteer, whether they can explain the offer in their own words, and whether the message attracts the audience you actually want. A promise can produce high traffic while bringing in people who expect an entirely different product.

When you do use paid traffic, keep the path coherent. Google’s [guidance on optimising ads and landing pages](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6238826?hl=en) emphasises that ads and landing pages should be relevant to each other and useful to visitors. That same principle applies to message testing: the promise in the ad, page, and next action should describe the same outcome. Otherwise a click may measure confusion rather than demand.

## Ask the question behind the conversion

After someone signs up, buys, or books a call, ask a short question: “What made this feel worth trying now?” Do the same when someone declines: “What did you expect this to help with?”

You will often hear a gap between the team’s intended message and the customer’s interpretation. That gap is valuable. It may mean the product needs a clearer explanation. It may mean the customer segment is different from the one you assumed. Or it may reveal a better message you would not have invented in isolation.

Resist the urge to turn every answer into proof. A handful of conversations can suggest a direction; they cannot establish a universal truth. Use them to create a better next test, then check whether the pattern persists.

## Scale only after the message earns the attention

Paid acquisition amplifies whatever is already true about your message. If the promise is fuzzy, you will pay to show fuzziness to more people. If it is precise but aimed at the wrong audience, you will produce tidy dashboards and poor-fit leads.

Before increasing spend, check four things:

1. The intended audience can repeat the value in their own words.
2. The landing page answers the question created by the message.
3. The next step feels proportionate to the promise.
4. Early users reach the outcome the message implied.

This does not make positioning permanent. Markets change, products develop, and audiences become more specific. But it gives growth work a solid starting point: language that was earned through contact with real people, not selected because it sounded polished in a slide deck.

The message that resonates is usually not hidden in a brainstorm. It is waiting in the customer’s description of a problem worth solving. Listen closely, make a clear promise, test it modestly, and scale only when the response gives you a real reason to believe.
