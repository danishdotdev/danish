## A landing page is not a product brochure

Early landing pages often try to hold every good thing a product could become: every feature, every audience, every future use case, every integration. The result is usually hard to understand because the visitor has to decide which part applies to them.

A better landing page begins with one question the right visitor is already asking:

> Can this help me solve this specific problem?

Everything on the page should make that answer easier to find.

## Choose one visitor and one situation

Do not start with demographics. Start with a situation.

“Teams” is broad. “A consultant who leaves client calls with messy notes and uncertain next steps” is a situation. It has a clear pain, a real moment of use, and a way to judge whether the product helps.

When the audience and situation are clear, the page can make a concrete promise: “Turn a client call into a reviewed follow-up and task list.” A visitor can immediately decide whether that is relevant.

## Answer the questions in order

A focused page should answer five things without making someone hunt:

1. **What is this?** State the outcome in plain language.
2. **Who is it for?** Name the situation or kind of work.
3. **How does it work?** Show a short, believable example.
4. **Why should I trust it?** Explain the boundaries, review step, proof, or source context that matters.
5. **What do I do now?** Offer one logical next action.

This is not a rigid wireframe. It is a check that the page is doing its basic communication job.

## Use one useful example instead of a feature wall

If the product helps transform meeting notes into follow-up work, show the before and after. If it helps someone prepare a customer brief, show the inputs, the draft, and the user’s review point.

An example gives the visitor a way to imagine their own work moving through the product. A feature list often makes them do the mental assembly themselves.

Google’s [landing-page guidance](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6238826?hl=en) makes a related point: the landing page should closely match the expectation created by the message that brought someone there. A concrete example is often the simplest way to make that match visible.

## Make the call to action match the decision

The page should not ask for a bigger commitment than it has earned. Someone who has just recognised a problem may be ready to see an example, request access, or book a short conversation. They may not be ready to “transform their business.”

Choose one primary action that fits the stage of understanding. Make it visible, name what happens next, and remove competing calls to action that pull attention away from the core decision.

## Remove anything that does not answer the question

Once the page has a clear promise, use it as a filter. Does a section make the outcome more believable? Does it help the visitor self-identify? Does it remove an important concern? If not, move it elsewhere or remove it.

Focus is not minimalism for its own sake. It is respect for a visitor’s attention. A good landing page makes one important choice easy: this product is worth a closer look, or it is not for me right now.
